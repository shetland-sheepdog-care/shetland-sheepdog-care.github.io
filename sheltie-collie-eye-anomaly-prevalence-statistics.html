Sheltie Collie Eye Anomaly: Check the Risk, Protect Their Future

Understand the records, ask better questions, and help protect the next generation of Shelties.

Updated October 8, 2026 · Source-checked draft · Human editorial review pending

Does Sheltie Collie Eye Anomaly really give your puppy a “30% risk,” or does that headline leave out the information you need? A percentage without a defined population, testing method, and date cannot tell you what is happening in your dog's eyes. Your more useful goal is to assemble reliable records and turn them into a screening plan that protects this puppy and informs future breeding decisions.

What does Sheltie Collie Eye Anomaly mean?

Collie eye anomaly, abbreviated CEA, describes abnormalities in how the eye develops. The OFA eye disease glossary defines a congenital syndrome that can include choroidal hypoplasia, coloboma, and retinal detachment. Choroidal hypoplasia involves an underdeveloped choroid, while a coloboma is a developmental absence of tissue. The glossary describes choroidal hypoplasia itself as nonprogressive.

That distinction matters when reading a diagnosis: the name of the syndrome and the particular findings in your dog belong together. Ask the examiner to write down the abnormality in each eye rather than settling for a broad label. If your Sheltie also has skin or muscle changes, keep those notes separate and compare them with our guide to Sheltie dermatomyositis symptoms and age of onset so two different health questions do not get mixed together. Keep that report beside your dog's identification details so another veterinarian can understand exactly what was found.

Is the “30% risk” claim reliable for your Sheltie?

This revision does not present 30% as a verified current, breed-wide estimate. The sources reviewed here do not establish that interpretation. Before accepting any prevalence claim, ask whether it counts dogs with visible abnormalities, dogs carrying a genetic variant, or dogs with the genotype associated with disease. Those questions describe different outcomes.

A 1982 UK study indexed in PubMed reported CEA in 72% of the Shetland Sheepdogs examined. The study examined approximately 2,500 dogs across Shetland Sheepdogs and two Collie breeds over three years. That historical examination sample is not a current American population estimate, and its reported percentage is not your puppy's personal probability of blindness.

When evaluating a statistic, write down the country, year, sample selection, and diagnostic method. The same habit helps with day-to-day care records too, including routines like the activity plan in our Sheltie exercise needs guide. A screened group may differ from the dogs you are considering. If a breeder quotes a reassuring number, ask for the actual report instead of debating the number in isolation. Your decision should rest on identifiable dogs and documented findings.

Can a dog with CEA still have normal vision?

Yes. The OFA overview of CEA describes outcomes ranging from no apparent visual impairment to blindness. It also warns that mildly affected dogs can produce puppies with severe disease. Shetland Sheepdogs are among the affected breeds, and an ophthalmologist can identify characteristic abnormalities in puppies as early as 6–8 weeks.

Normal play at home is therefore a useful observation to share, but it is not a clearance certificate. Conversely, an abnormal report deserves a discussion about your dog's actual vision and specific findings rather than an assumption that all affected dogs have the same future. Ask what the diagnosis means for this dog, what follow-up is appropriate, and what changes should prompt another examination.

What do an eye examination and DNA test each reveal?

The two reports answer complementary questions. According to OFA's eye certification guidance, a CAER examination evaluates observable eye findings, and a clinically normal animal may still carry a genetic abnormality. Board-certified veterinary ophthalmologists perform these screening examinations. A screening certificate is not a comprehensive assessment of every possible eye problem.

Compare the registered names or permanent identification on the paperwork with the dogs being discussed. Save original files rather than relying on a message saying “eyes clear.” Clear on an examination and clear for a tested variant should be explained separately.

What do clear, carrier, and affected DNA results mean?

The UC Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory describes the tested form as autosomal recessive. N/N means the tested allele was not detected; N/CEA means carrier; CEA/CEA means two copies were detected. Carriers are not affected by this inherited form, but can transmit the allele. For two carriers, the predicted affected-puppy proportion is 25%; this is a genetic expectation, not a promise about a particular litter.

The laboratory also explains that the tested deletion does not account for every optic nerve coloboma. Use the DNA report alongside clinical examination, rather than treating it as an all-purpose eye guarantee. Ask your veterinarian or a qualified genetics adviser to interpret a planned pairing before breeding.

Keep the distinction between breeding advice and the care of an existing pet clear in your own notes. A carrier result is information to preserve and discuss; it is not a reason to regard a companion as less deserving of care. Responsible record sharing can help future owners make informed choices without turning a dog's genetic status into a judgment about its worth.

Which changes should prompt a veterinary call?

New difficulty navigating deserves attention. PetMD's veterinary guide to CEA lists bumping into objects, difficulty with stairs, and trouble locating familiar items among possible signs of impaired vision. It explains that CEA cannot be reversed, although specialist treatment may help certain complications, including some partial retinal detachments.

If vision changes suddenly, contact a veterinarian promptly rather than using this article or a home test to identify the cause. Describe when the change began, what your dog did, and whether it affects familiar surroundings. Ask the clinic how urgently your dog should be assessed. Avoid putting the dog through obstacle courses or moving furniture to “test” eyesight.

While awaiting advice, keep the surroundings predictable and block access to obvious hazards. For a dog with a confirmed diagnosis, ask the ophthalmologist for an individual monitoring plan. Write down the clinic's instructions in plain language so everyone caring for the dog knows what to watch for and whom to contact.

How should owners turn CEA records into a long-term care file?

The purpose of this article is not to make a Sheltie owner afraid of every eye diagnosis. The useful goal is a durable record system that follows the dog through puppyhood, adulthood, rehoming, breeding decisions, or specialist referrals. Keep the ophthalmology report, DNA result, microchip or registration details, and follow-up instructions in one folder. Name the files clearly, because a screenshot titled “clear eyes” is much less useful than a report tied to an identifiable dog.

If you are comparing puppies, ask the breeder which documents belong to the puppy and which belong to the parents. If you already own the dog, ask your veterinarian whether the record changes everyday care, future monitoring, or breeding advice. That small administrative habit protects more than one dog: it helps your current companion receive appropriate care, and it helps future Sheltie families avoid decisions based on rumor or an unsupported percentage.

How does screening help the next generation?

Your contribution can be modest and practical: preserve accurate reports, share them with the people responsible for your dog's care, and ask for documented screening before choosing a puppy or planning a mating. The aim is better decisions and a good life for the dog in front of you.

Make a folder containing original reports, the dog's identification, appointment notes, and the agreed follow-up plan. When you hand responsibility to a sitter or another family member, share the care instructions they need. For Sheltie Collie Eye Anomaly, that steady habit is more useful than responding to an alarming percentage without context.

Frequently asked questions about Sheltie CEA

Does every Sheltie with CEA become blind?

No. Visual outcomes vary, and some affected dogs have no apparent impairment. Ask the ophthalmologist about the findings in your individual dog.

Does 30% describe my puppy’s chance of blindness?

No verified current breed-wide estimate supporting that interpretation was identified in this review. A prevalence percentage is not an individual prognosis.

When can an ophthalmologist identify CEA?

Characteristic abnormalities can be identified as early as 6–8 weeks. Discuss the appropriate appointment timing with your veterinarian.

Is a carrier the same as an affected dog?

No. UC Davis distinguishes N/CEA carriers from dogs with two copies of the tested allele. A carrier can pass the allele to offspring.

Does a normal eye examination replace DNA testing?

No. An examination records observable findings; a clinically normal dog may carry a genetic variant. Discuss both reports with your veterinarian.

Can CEA be cured?

CEA cannot be reversed. An ophthalmologist can assess whether treatment for particular complications is appropriate.

Best Brushes for a Sheltie Coat: Pin Brush Pin Length, Slicker vs Comb, and a Weekly Brushing Kit Compared

A pin brush, a slicker, a comb and a rake each do one job well and another job badly. Here is how the tools compare on a Shetland Sheepdog, which pin length to buy, and how to put them together into a kit you will actually use every week.

Grooming • Tool comparison • 13 min read

Picking the best brushes for a Sheltie coat is harder than it looks, because a Sheltie wears two coats at once: a long, harsh outer layer and a soft, packed undercoat underneath. A tool that glides through one layer often skates over the other or snags in it. This comparison weighs each option against the alternatives, so you can stop buying brushes that end up in a drawer.

Quick answer: For most adult Shelties, the core kit is a long-pin brush (around 20–27 mm pins) for the body coat, a steel greyhound-style comb to check that you have reached the skin, and a slicker or undercoat rake for shedding season. Use a short-pin or small pin brush for puppies, legs and behind the ears. A slicker is not a replacement for a comb: the slicker removes loose coat near the surface, while the comb is the only tool that proves a section is tangle-free all the way down. A deshedding blade is optional and, if used at all, should be used lightly.

Why Does a Sheltie Coat Need More Than One Brush?

Because the two layers behave differently. The outer coat is long and harsh, the undercoat is short and dense, and no single tool handles both well. You need one tool that reaches through, one that pulls loose undercoat, and one that checks your work.

What the breed standard says about the coat

The Royal Kennel Club breed standard for the Shetland Sheepdog describes the coat as double: an outer coat of long, harsh-textured, straight hair over an undercoat that is soft, short and close. It adds that the mane and frill are very abundant, the forelegs well feathered, and the hindlegs above the hocks profusely covered with hair. Those are exactly the zones where tangles start: the ruff, the feathering behind the front legs and the thick “pants” on the back legs.

Where single-tool routines go wrong

A brush pulled over the top of the outer coat makes a Sheltie look tidy while the undercoat underneath keeps packing down. Owners usually discover the problem in spring or autumn, when the loose undercoat has nowhere to go and felts into clumps behind the ears or in the pants. That is why almost every experienced Sheltie owner ends up with a small kit rather than one “perfect” brush.

What Pin Length Does a Sheltie Pin Brush Need?

For an adult body coat, choose pins in the 20–27 mm range; go to the longer end for a heavy coat. Use short pins (around 16–20 mm) for puppies, legs and small areas.

Pin brush makers size their brushes by pin length. In its guide to why pin length matters, the grooming-tool maker Chris Christensen gives two general rules: the longer the coat, the longer the pin, and the harder the coat, the firmer the pad. It groups short and medium pins at 16–22 mm and says they suit most double coats, because the body coat on those breeds is generally shorter. Long pins run from 27 to 35 mm and are aimed at long drop coats.

Why Shelties sit between the two groups

A Sheltie is a double-coated breed, which points to short or medium pins, but its outer coat is long and the ruff and pants can be deep. That puts most adult Shelties at the boundary. In practice:

  • 20 mm is a comfortable all-rounder for a lighter or shorter adult coat and for the sides of the body.
  • 27 mm reaches deeper into a heavy ruff, the pants and any adult coat with a thick, soft undercoat.
  • 16 mm is gentle and useful on puppies and very sensitive dogs, but short pins can skim the surface of a full adult coat.
  • 35 mm is built for long, flowing drop coats and is more brush than most pet Shelties need.

One owner on Sheltie Forums' “Favorite Comb” thread described exactly this trade-off: they bought a 16 mm wooden pin brush because their sensitive dog did not tolerate grooming tools well, loved how gentle it was, and still wished they had bought the 20 or 27 mm version with longer pins.

Pin tips, pad and shape

  • Tips: look for smooth, rounded or polished pin ends. Sharp-cut ends scratch, and a Sheltie that feels scratched learns to avoid the brush.
  • Pad: a softer cushion flexes over bony areas; a firmer pad pushes harder into a harsh coat.
  • Shape: the same Chris Christensen guide describes the oval shape as the workhorse for large areas, the oblong for straightening coat and working behind ears, toes and armpits, and the pocket size for small areas and small dogs. Shelties vary in size, so check our guide to how big Shelties get compared with the breed standards before choosing between a full oval and a smaller head.

Slicker vs Comb: Which Should You Use on a Sheltie?

Use both, for different jobs. A slicker pulls loose undercoat and fluff from the surface layers; a comb is the test that tells you whether a section is clear all the way to the skin.

What a slicker does well, and what it does badly

A slicker has many short, bent wire pins on a cushion. It is fast at lifting loose undercoat from the pants, the feathering and the tail, and many owners use it for most of the routine. In the same Sheltie Forums thread, one owner said a good-quality slicker and a rake are their best tools for undercoat, and that the slicker does double duty 95% of the time. The weakness is depth and pressure: a slicker works the top layers, and pressed hard it rakes across the skin. Hold it loosely, use short strokes from the wrist, and let the pins do the work.

What a comb does that a slicker cannot

A steel greyhound-style comb, with wider teeth at one end and finer teeth at the other, is not a fast tool. Its job is honesty. If the comb glides from skin to tip, that section is done; if it catches, there is still a tangle or a pad of undercoat, however good the coat looks on top. Use the wide end through the ruff and pants and the fine end behind the ears and on the feathering.

So which one first?

For a weekly line brush, work each section with the pin brush or slicker, then pass the comb through it before moving on. In shedding season, run the rake or slicker over the coat first to remove the bulk of loose undercoat, then line brush. That order keeps the pin brush from clogging and saves your wrist.

Do You Need an Undercoat Rake for a Sheltie?

Not every week, but most owners are glad to have one when the coat blows. A rake has one or two rows of long teeth that reach into the undercoat and pull out what is already loose.

Rake vs pin brush during a shed

During a heavy shed, a pin brush fills with undercoat after a few strokes. A rake removes the bulk faster, after which the pin brush and comb can finish the job. Outside shedding season, the rake can stay in the drawer for most of the week.

Using a rake without overdoing it

  • Work in the direction the coat grows, never against it.
  • Stop on a section once the rake comes out with little hair; more passes take good coat, not loose coat.
  • Keep strokes short in the ruff and pants, where the undercoat is thickest.
  • Follow every raked section with the comb to see what is left.

How Do You Line Brush a Sheltie With This Kit?

Lay the dog on its side, part the coat in a line, brush the hair below the part down and out, then move the part up a little and repeat. Comb each section before you move on.

  1. Mist first. Lightly spray the section with water or a diluted brushing spray. Brushing a bone-dry coat can break the outer hairs and builds static.
  2. Start low. Begin at the lower back leg with the dog lying on its side. Hold the coat above a horizontal line out of the way with your free hand.
  3. Brush the line. With the pin brush, brush the exposed hair down and away from the body, from the skin outward, in short strokes.
  4. Comb the line. Run the comb through the same strip. If it catches, go back with the slicker or pin brush, holding the hair at the base so you do not pull on the skin.
  5. Move the part up. Let down a thin layer of coat, brush and comb it, and keep going until you reach the spine. Then turn the dog and do the other side.
  6. Finish the friction spots. Use the small comb or pocket brush behind the ears, under the elbows and around the collar line.

If your dog is still learning to lie still for this, keep sessions short and stop on a good moment. Our step-by-step plan for teaching a Sheltie puppy to accept brushing works for older dogs that are new to line brushing too.

The Weekly Sheltie Brushing Kit: Checklist

This is the routine most adult pet Shelties can live with. Adjust it with the kit builder above.

Once a week: the full line brush

  • ☐ Spray bottle filled and the coat lightly misted.
  • ☐ Pin brush through every section of the body, layer by layer.
  • ☐ Comb through every section to the skin.
  • ☐ Slicker over the pants, feathering and tail.
  • ☐ Fine end of the comb behind the ears.

Two to four times a week: quick checks

  • ☐ Comb behind both ears.
  • ☐ Check under the elbows and where the collar or harness sits.
  • ☐ Quick slicker pass over the pants.

In a heavy shed

  • ☐ Rake or slicker first, outdoors if possible, to remove the bulk.
  • ☐ Then the normal line brush and comb.
  • ☐ Add an extra session while tufts are still coming out.

Brushing changes how a collar or harness sits in the ruff, so re-check the fit after a big brush-out; our guide to choosing between a martingale and a harness for a Sheltie explains the two-finger check. And if grooming day comes with a bored, wriggly dog, a walk or a game first helps; see how many minutes of exercise a Sheltie needs each day.

Common Brushing Mistakes With a Sheltie Coat

  • Brushing only the top. The outer coat looks finished while the undercoat packs down underneath. Line brush, then comb.
  • Buying pins that are too short. A 16 mm brush is lovely on a puppy and skims the surface of a full adult coat.
  • Pressing hard with a slicker. Many Shelties that “hate brushing” hate being scraped. Light, short strokes.
  • Skipping the comb. Without it, you are guessing whether a section is clear.
  • Brushing a dry coat. A light mist reduces breakage and static.
  • Raking until the tool comes out empty. After the loose undercoat is gone, more passes thin the good coat.
  • Leaving the ears and pants for last. They tangle fastest, so check them first when time is short.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best brush for a Sheltie if I can only buy one?

A good pin brush with 20 to 27 mm rounded pins, used in layers, does the most work on its own. Add a steel greyhound-style comb as soon as you can, because it is the only way to check you have reached the skin.

Is a slicker brush or a pin brush better for a Sheltie?

They do different jobs. A pin brush is better for line brushing the long outer coat; a slicker is faster for lifting loose undercoat from the pants, feathering and tail. Most owners use the pin brush weekly and the slicker for quick passes and shedding season.

What pin length should a Sheltie pin brush have?

For an adult coat, 20 to 27 mm. Grooming-tool maker Chris Christensen puts most double coats in the 16 to 22 mm range and long coats at 27 to 35 mm; a Sheltie's long outer coat and deep ruff place it between the two. Use 16 to 20 mm for puppies and small areas.

Do Shelties need an undercoat rake?

Not for every session, but a rake is the fastest way to remove bulk undercoat when the coat is shedding heavily. Rake in the direction of growth, stop once little hair comes out, and comb afterwards.

Should I use a deshedding blade tool on my Sheltie?

Many Sheltie owners avoid blade-style deshedding tools or use them only briefly, because the blade can cut or break the harsh outer coat. A rake and a slicker remove loose undercoat with less risk to the coat.

How long does a full Sheltie brush-out take?

It depends on the coat and the dog, but for a full adult coat plan on most of an hour for a thorough line brush, and longer in a heavy shed. Splitting it into two or three shorter sessions is fine, especially for a dog that gets restless.

The Bottom Line

The best brushes for a Sheltie coat are a small team rather than one star: a 20–27 mm pin brush for the body, a steel comb to prove you reached the skin, a slicker for the pants and feathering, and a rake for shedding season. Choose pin length by your dog's coat, keep the pressure light, and comb every section before you move on. Run the kit builder again whenever the season or the coat changes, and the weekly brush-out stays a short, manageable job instead of a monthly battle.
